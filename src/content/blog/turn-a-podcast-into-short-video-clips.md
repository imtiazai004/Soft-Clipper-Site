<p class="answer">The hardest part of turning a podcast into short vertical clips usually isn't finding the right moments — it's that most podcast recordings don't have real video to cut from in the first place. Fix that, and the rest of the process is close to any other long-form video: pick the moment, frame it, caption it. Skip it, and no clipping tool can manufacture footage that was never recorded.</p>

## Most podcasts still don't record video

According to the Independent Podcast Report (2025), only 31% of podcast creators currently publish full video episodes alongside their audio. Another 32% don't yet but are considering it, and 19% have no plans to at all. If you're in either of the latter two groups, that's the first decision to make before any clipping workflow matters: without a webcam or camera recording running during the interview, there's no footage for a clipper — AI or manual — to work from.

The fallback some shows use is an audiogram: a static image or waveform animation behind the audio, exported as a "video." It technically satisfies a platform's video requirement, but it isn't the same thing as a real clip — there's no face, no reaction, nothing for a viewer's eye to track, which is a real disadvantage on feeds where every other clip has an actual person talking. If short-form clips are part of the plan for a show, recording video during the session (Zoom, Riverside, StreamYard, or a camera pointed at each host) costs nothing extra to set up and is the difference between having real source material later and not.

Worth noting for context: YouTube is now the primary podcast platform for 40% of listeners, per Sounds Profitable's 2025 Podcast Landscape Study — but even among that group, nearly half say more than half of their actual listening is still audio-only. Video adoption is genuinely growing, not universal, and most people still describe themselves as podcast *listeners* first.

## Interview framing is a different problem than solo video

Once there's video to work with, a podcast clip runs into a framing issue that a single-person talking-head video doesn't: two or more people, and the crop has to follow whoever is actually speaking without looking like it's guessing. A centre crop that just keeps the middle of a wide two-shot cuts one guest's face in half. Auto-tracking that follows only one speaker leaves the other person invisible for their own lines. The fix that actually holds up for interviews is a split-frame stack — both speakers cropped to their own face and stacked vertically — rather than a single tracked crop trying to follow a back-and-forth conversation. This is covered in more detail, including the other common reframing failures, in [why auto-reframed clips look wrong](/blog/why-auto-reframing-looks-wrong/).

## Picking moments in a conversation

The general process for turning any long recording into short clips — read the transcript, mark moments that stand alone, cut the first few seconds ruthlessly — is the same one covered in [turning a long video into shorts](/blog/turn-long-videos-into-shorts/), and it applies here too. What's specific to interviews is the shape of what to look for: a complete question-and-answer exchange rather than half of one, a disagreement where both people's points are audible, or a story one guest tells start to finish without the other host needing to fill in context. Watch for the interview-specific version of a bad clip opening — not just "so, as I was saying," but the round of "yeah," "totally," "right, right" that hosts and guests trade before someone actually starts answering. That handoff chatter is almost never worth keeping at the start of a clip.

## The transcript is only as good as the noisiest mic

Every clipping step downstream — which moments get suggested, how clean the captions are — starts from a transcript, and a podcast interview is more likely than a solo video to have mismatched audio: a host on a proper studio microphone, a remote guest dialling in on a laptop or phone. The louder, cleaner track transcribes well; the noisier one is where mistakes creep in — a misheard name, a dropped word, a caption that doesn't quite match what was said. It's worth spot-checking the transcript specifically around the guest's lines before trusting it, rather than assuming accuracy is even across both speakers. The broader case for proofreading before you burn in captions is covered in [how to add captions to a video](/blog/how-to-add-captions-to-video/).
