Short answer: YouTube Studio has a solid built-in tool for captions and subtitles — it is not a text-overlay editor, and the two get confused often enough to be worth separating.

## What it covers

Through the Subtitles tab in YouTube Studio, you can:

- Generate automatic captions using YouTube's speech-recognition system, in 100+ languages
- Type or paste a full transcript and have it auto-synced to the video's timing
- Upload a subtitle file directly if you already have one
- Edit any line of the captions after they're generated
- Add non-speech descriptive text for accessibility, like "[applause]" or "[music playing]"
- Publish subtitles in multiple languages on the same video

That's the captions job handled completely on its own, no extra software needed if standard captions are all that's wanted.

## What it doesn't cover

YouTube's own documentation is focused entirely on subtitle and caption management, and stops there. It does not include:

- **Styling or positioning captions** beyond what's already in an uploaded caption file — no dragging text around the frame or picking a font/colour inside Studio itself
- **On-screen text overlays** that aren't captions — a title card, a callout, a word appearing over a clip for emphasis
- **Any other visual editing** — graphics, transitions, stickers, or the word-by-word highlighted caption style common on short-form video

If the goal is styled, positioned, attention-grabbing text on screen — not just standard bottom-of-frame subtitles — that's outside what the built-in editor is built for.

## Auto-captions still need a review pass

Worth flagging regardless of which tool generates them: YouTube's own guidance is that automatic captions "might misrepresent the spoken content due to mispronunciations, accents, dialects, or background noise," and recommends reviewing and editing before publishing. Accuracy also drops with poor audio, overlapping speakers, or multiple languages in one video. Treat auto-captions as a first draft, not a final one, on YouTube or anywhere else.

## When it's not enough

Short-form content usually wants the punchy, word-timed caption style common on TikTok, Reels and Shorts — burned directly into the frame with styling, rather than a plain subtitle track. That's a different job to what YouTube Studio's Subtitles tab is built for.
